Working notes · status as of 28 November 2026

How low can the polynomial go?

On 24 November 2026 an OpenAI preprint announced a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, with a Lean development behind it. Its running time is a polynomial of very high degree. This project works on lowering that degree.

One rule: an argument counts only if it is formalized in Lean. Everything else on this page is labelled as an estimate.

Orientation

Three objects, and which one each result is about

The preprint's text and its Lean development are not the same algorithm with the same constants, so every number on this page says which of them it refers to.

01 · the preprint

The text

Reduces LP to side decisions, and each side decision to a search for prices from sign labels (its Theorem 2). Labels come from a feasibility test; the numbers involved are shortened by an online Frank–Tardos style compression.

Stated cost of the price search: about N60 log(NR) label queries.

02 · upstream Lean

OpenAI's formalization

Proves the end-to-end theorem on a machine model. It formalizes its own, cruder variant of the search, with much larger constants: 2600+1 probes per node and a query bound x1510.

This project builds it locally and changes it only through patches that still compile to its top-level theorems.

03 · this repository

The SPLP library

An independent Lean formalization of the preprint's price search with every numerical constant as a parameter, plus the parts of the label test, calibration and compression needed to shrink their constants.

Self-contained: it depends on Mathlib only.

How to read the labels

  • ✓ Lean theoremProved in this repository's library. No sorry, standard axioms only.
  • ⚙ Compiled patchA patch to upstream's Lean development, after which its top-level theorems strongly_polynomial_optimization and strongly_polynomial_feasibility still build with standard axioms.
  • ~ Not formalizedAn estimate, a simulation or a reading of the text. Not checked by a compiler and not to be relied on.
  • ✕ Ruled outAn idea that fails, with a counterexample theorem where one exists.

Results

Eight results, in the order they were found

Each links to the Lean file that states it. The README has the full statements and every caveat.

1

The price search needs (2N)46 label queries, not N60

The algorithm is the preprint's: the same experts, markets, feedback rule and recursion. The saving comes from the accounting. Gains and losses are kept as exact logarithms, reserve masses follow a free geometric sequence, the bonus per failed probe is chosen so that its inequality is tight, and the constants are re-optimized. Checking a new set of constants is one norm_num call.

2

What a market step has to handle

The pool of experts is exponentially large, so a market step can only work on aggregates. After t probes the experts of one group fall into at most O(tk) cells with common wealth and common best options, because each probe cuts ℝk by a fixed number of hyperplanes. The product of the preprint's interval partitions would give degree 15 in t for arity 4, against degree 4.

Three changes to the algorithm are proved correct with the same query bounds. Wealth can be rounded to 41 significant bits, where exact wealth grows by about 21 bits per probe. Markets can be cleared approximately, with a tolerance that is a constant. And only the aggregate gain matters: a proposal is certified by N signed slot masses whose absolute values sum to at most 2−25 of the wealth.

✓ Lean theorem

3

The whole market buys exactly one thing: R → log R

In the preprint's model, with integer demands in [−R, R], a trivial algorithm works: move every price by ½ in the direction of its label. After 2R queries every demand of every consistent hypothesis is met. No experts and no markets.

So the price problem is easy when cost may be linear in R, and everything the recursive market achieves is the replacement of R by log R. For LP that is the difference between polynomial and strongly polynomial, since R is exponential in N after compression.

✓ Lean theorem
  • lockstep_2R
  • descent_meets_demands
4

The range cannot be reduced by rounding

Scaling would be the largest saving available: solve a coarse problem, then refine. It fails. A rounded problem needs labels that are correct within a margin as large as the rounding, and a margin G costs either 2G queries or a full price search of range G.

With exact labels it is impossible outright: there are two hypotheses and one label rule consistent with both, whose rounded demands need opposite labels at the same point.

✓ Lean theorem✕ Ruled out
  • RoundingExample.no_common_label
  • consistentAt_of_margin_of_round
5

The label test is more reliable than the search assumes

The search only assumes that labels are right at slots of maximum surplus. The test that produces them does better: if it passes at a coordinate, every true witness is invisible there by a factor (N−1)ε/θ, so labels are right within a margin that grows linearly with the bits of precision. With zero tolerance the test is never wrong.

Whether that margin survives calibration and compression has not been checked, and by results 3 and 4 a margin does not lower the exponent.

✓ Lean theorem
  • falsePositive_bound_max
  • exact_test_sound
6

The compression constant, here and in upstream's development

The range R is not a property of the linear program. It is the size of the short substitutes that Section 5 computes for logarithms of minors, and it is governed by one constant D, a bound on dissociated sets of minors. The preprint fixes D = (d+1)6. Lemma 5.1 is formalized here with 12d²⌈log2(d+1)⌉, and log R is shown to be of order D4K for the preprint's formulas.

The same change is carried into upstream's Lean development by three patches, applied in order (width, search constants, precision radius). After all of them its two top-level theorems build (1,552 modules) with standard axioms.

7

The precision constants can be much smaller

The preprint sets its precision through H = 2100N². Its own proof of Theorem 3 derives a coefficient bound 3j−1 and then relaxes it. Formalized here: the bound 3j for rows of the form calibration produces, Lemma 3.1 with a tolerance of order 1/N in place of 2−1000N², and soundness of the label test at a precision linear in N.

A second factor of N would come from the observation that the inequalities of Section 4.3 are difference constraints. The criterion for difference constraints and the one-sidedness of cofactor vectors are proved. That Section 4.3 has exactly this form is still a reading of the text.

8

One short strategy, in upstream's own query model

Results 1 and 2 say that every valid run is correct and short. Here the search becomes a single adaptive strategy: a tree whose next query depends only on the answers so far. For upstream's unchanged definition of the price game there is one tree, correct for every hidden instance, of height at most

N · 2 · (186473 + 70784 ln N + 50560 ln(R+2)) · (2N)46.

Upstream's own strategy in that model has the bound x1510. The tree is only shown to exist in the query model. Its proposals are chosen non-constructively, and it is not connected to upstream's machine-model layer.

✓ Lean theorem

  • exists_strategy_k4

Dead ends

What does not work, and why

Most of the effort after the first results went into looking for a structurally cheaper market step. None was found. The reasons are recorded so that nobody has to find them twice.

Scaling the range

Rounded demands need labels with a margin as large as the rounding, so each scale costs a full price search. Result 4.

✓ Lean theorem

Children that return one number

If a child node returned the same multiple of every expert's input, the charge a failed child must pay could not be met. The proof does survive a thresholded return, but the experts above the threshold can hold most of the wealth, so the cell count does not drop.

✓ Lean theorem

Dynamic programming over pairwise comparisons

The factor applied to an expert depends on which of its options is best. A function of the index of the maximum that factors over pairwise comparisons is constant, so the wealth is not a pairwise product model.

✓ Lean theorem

Sparse samples of experts

The error of a weighted sample on ranges grows by a constant factor per update. Only closeness in total variation is stable under multiplicative updates, and such approximations are not sparse.

~ Not formalized

Simpler experts

Experts that bet on every slot above a threshold, or k single-slot experts in place of one, break the analysis: feedback is guaranteed only above the child's threshold, and the average log gain turns negative.

~ Not formalized

Independent per-slot pools

A product-form market over single guesses would need no recursion and O(N log R) queries. In simulation, labels that push non-maximal coordinates away keep it from converging even for N = 2.

~ Not formalized

Approximate minimizers

A point that nearly minimizes the potential in value need not satisfy the gain condition. The condition is about slopes, not values.

~ Not formalized

Learning the maximum surplus

The value of the maximum surplus cannot be obtained from the label oracle, and log R queries are needed already for N = 2. The range can only be shrunk at its source, in the compression constants.

~ Not formalized

Current directions

In progress and open

Three tracks are running as of 28 November 2026. None has a verified result yet; this section will be updated when they land or fail.

In progress

Upstream's w8 bit formula

Upstream bounds the bit length of compressed values by about 80w8 in the width w. The preprint's text gives order w4K, at most about w5. So upstream's formal chain loses about three powers of w, each worth a factor N³.

Goal: find where and recover what the existing proofs allow.

In progress

Upstream's search constants

The 2557 probes per node are pinned by a charging constant 2−272 that comes from 64-coordinate skeletons. The preprint's text uses arity 4 and 219 probes.

Goal: retune the parameters consistently inside upstream's proof architecture.

In progress

The N² log N width, end to end

The smaller width compiles for the mathematical core, but upstream's machine-model layer wants a polynomial bit budget, and a logarithm is not one.

Goal: scope whether the algorithm can run with the smaller width while the machine layer keeps a polynomial over-estimate.

  1. A market step whose cost does not grow with the number of probes

    The total cost is roughly (probes)k+1, so one point of probe exponent costs five in the total for arity 4. Fewer cells, or cells that can be merged or forgotten, would matter more than any further constant tuning. Within this family of algorithms the tk count looks robust. The one remaining lead is a data structure: the wealth evolves by range updates on the cells of a tropical arrangement, and partition trees with lazy updates might save one factor of t. Unverified.

  2. An explicit cost model for the market step

    Two pieces are missing between "what a market step has to handle" and a running time: the number of potential evaluations needed to reach a vertex minimizer, and the cost of counting the experts in a cell. Also open: a procedure that produces the approximate weights the tolerance theorem allows.

  3. Close the gaps in the precision argument

    Formalize that Section 4.3 of the preprint is exactly a system of two-slot comparisons, that calibration's rows have the form the coefficient bound assumes, and that the smaller constant D propagates through Lemmas 5.2 to 5.4. Until then the estimates log R ≈ N11 and N9 stay estimates.

  4. Connect the short strategy to a machine model

    The (2N)46 tree exists in upstream's query model but its proposals are not computed. Upstream's machine-model layer simulates its own variant of the search, so replacing that variant is not a local change.

  5. An end-to-end exponent

    Nothing here multiplies the levels of the ledger together in one theorem. That needs the cost model above and a formalization of calibration, compression and the reduction from LP at the sharper constants.

Scope

What this project does not claim

  • It does not re-verify the preprint. Upstream's Lean development is taken as given and built locally; the patches here only change constants inside it.
  • There is no machine model in this repository's library. Runs are mathematical objects, and the cost statements bound the size of what must be handled, not the time of an implementation.
  • There is no end-to-end exponent for linear programming.
  • The figure "about 60" for the preprint is its own stated bound. Only the arithmetic behind it is checked here.
  • Comparisons between arities compare proved upper bounds. No lower bound on any cost is proved.
  • The Lean proofs were written by Claude, an AI model, working under direction. Their correctness rests on the Lean kernel, not on the author; the estimates have no such backing.
